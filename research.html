Research & collaborations

Five studies of the neurovascular unit, all read out of public electron microscopy volumes rather than collected at a microscope. That is what makes them possible at all here: there is no laboratory behind this site and no funding — a volume somebody else has already imaged and released is something one person can work through in their spare time, which is what this is.

Each study below carries what we found, the figure, and the coordinates the figure was taken at — so you can open the same place in the tools on this site and look for yourself.

The five studies

In the order they are easiest to read, not the order they were written. Two preprints among them are not yet peer-reviewed, and say so.

Single author · peer-reviewed

Ultrastructure of precapillary sphincters and the neurovascular unit

Søren Grubb

Vascular Biology 5(1), e230011 (2023) · doi:10.1530/VB-23-0011

The paper that started the tools. A precapillary sphincter is a ring-shaped narrowing where a penetrating arteriole gives off its first capillary, and almost nothing was known about what it is made of. Reading it in a volume meant every cell of the neurovascular unit could be looked at in the same place at once.

What we found

  • At the sphincter the mural cell is not a band but several layers of processes wrapped around the indentation — against a single layer on the arteriole above it and on the capillary below.
  • Endothelial pinocytic vesicles are densest immediately downstream of the sphincter (≈10/µm² on first-order capillaries against ≈3.5/µm² on the arteriole), and denser on branch points that have a sphincter than on those that do not.
  • Collagen fibrils made by perivascular fibroblasts reinforce the proximal first-order capillary, where the pressure drop is steepest.
  • Neuronal processes lie perpendicular to the vessel around penetrating arterioles and arterial-end capillaries — in mouse, and again in the human h01 volume — but not around ascending venules.

With Hobart · peer-reviewed

Microglia contact cerebral vasculature through gaps between astrocyte endfeet

Gary P. Morris, Catherine G. Foster, Brad A. Sutherland & Søren Grubb

Journal of Cerebral Blood Flow & Metabolism 44(12), 1472–1486 (2024) · doi:10.1177/0271678X241280775

Astrocyte endfeet are usually described as covering the vessel completely. They do not quite: there are gaps between neighbouring endfeet, and the question was whether anything uses them. Three regions of interest were cut through the volume — one around a venule, one around an arteriole, one through the capillary bed between them — and every microglial nucleus in them was annotated by hand.

What we found

  • Of 307 microglia annotated, 21.4% (63 of 295 scored) touch the vessel directly through a gap between adjacent astrocyte endfeet. We called the contacts plugs.
  • A plug-forming microglia makes a median of 2 and as many as 17, almost all within a few micrometres of the soma; the closer the cell sits to the vessel, the more it makes.
  • Plugs are enriched at the venule end of the tree, and contact endothelial cells over pericytes 3:1.
  • They range from 0.01 µm² to about 15 µm², but 95% are under 1 µm² — small enough that light microscopy would not have separated them from a near miss. 61.8% are bounded by two endfeet and 33.9% by three.

With the NIH and Janelia · preprint

Pericyte and endothelial primary cilia and centrioles have disparate organization across the brain microvasculature

Søren Grubb*, Vanshika Chaddha*, Jennifer Lippincott-Schwartz, Carolyn M. Ott & Amreen Mughal

bioRxiv 2025.11.19.689283 (2025) — preprint, not yet peer-reviewed · doi:10.1101/2025.11.19.689283

A primary cilium is a single antenna a cell puts out to feel its surroundings. Whether the cells of the brain's blood vessels have one, and where, had never been surveyed — because you need a volume to see a structure that is one micrometre long and attached to a cell you must first identify. Three volumes were read: MICrONS mouse visual cortex, a mouse hippocampal CA1 volume and the human h01 temporal cortex, plus nine other tissues.

What we found

  • Human cortical endothelial cells carry primary cilia, protruding into the lumen. Not one mouse endothelial cell in either mouse volume did — a species difference, not a sampling accident.
  • Mural cell cilia follow a gradient along the vascular tree: 83% of pial venular and 92% of ascending venular mural cells have one, against 3% of pial arteriolar and under 1% of penetrating arteriolar.
  • Three ways of building the base were found, including a partial pocket — some distal appendages at the plasma membrane and the rest on an invaginating one — in 25–50% of mural cells. Photoreceptors are the only other cell known to do this.
  • In mouse, about 75% of mural cell cilia point away from the vessel; most are ensheathed by an astrocyte endfoot, with the tip pressed into the astrocyte, and a few reach into the neuropil and pass close to synapses. In human, nearly all stay inside the basal lamina against the endothelium.
  • Endothelial centrioles sit consistently on the upstream side of the nucleus, against the direction of flow — which makes them a structural marker for which way the blood is going.

With Copenhagen and Boston · preprint

Distinct Ca²⁺ signatures of leptomeningeal fibroblast subgroups in awake mouse brains in health and inflammation

Chen He, Søren Grubb, Lechan Tao, Kıvılcım Kılıç, Xiao Zhang, Anna Devor, Neha Zahoor Sheikh & Changsi Cai

bioRxiv 2025.05.13.653681 (2025) — preprint, not yet peer-reviewed · doi:10.1101/2025.05.13.653681

The leptomeninges are the two thin sheets between skull and cortex, built mostly of fibroblasts. Two-photon imaging in awake mice can watch them signal but cannot tell the groups apart with any confidence; the ultrastructure can. The pial surface is not segmented in the public MICrONS release, so this half of the paper is a hand-traced reconstruction: a CloudVolume cut-out around one pial arteriole, segmented cell by cell in TrakEM2.

What we found

  • Five kinds of fibroblast, told apart by position and ultrastructure: arachnoid barrier cells, fibroblast reticular cells and inner arachnoid fibroblasts in the arachnoid mater, pia mater fibroblasts on the surface, and perivascular fibroblasts wrapped around the pial vessels.
  • Each group has its own Ca²⁺ profile at rest in awake mice, and pia mater fibroblasts carry the largest transients.
  • Vasodilation during locomotion is followed by a fibroblast Ca²⁺ rise that depends on TRPV4 — a channel opened by stretch, in the order you would expect if the vessel moves first.
  • Seven days after a systemic LPS challenge, spontaneous Ca²⁺ in pia mater fibroblasts falls, and macrophages have infiltrated every leptomeningeal layer.

Single author · preprint

Ultrastructure of the brain waste-clearance pathway

Søren Grubb

bioRxiv 2023.04.14.536712 (2023) — preprint, not yet peer-reviewed · doi:10.1101/2023.04.14.536712

Where does the brain's waste actually leave? The argument is usually between lymphatic vessels and the arachnoid villi, and it is usually made with tracers. This is the same question asked of the ultrastructure instead: follow the cells that carry waste and see where they put it down. Observational, and meant to generate hypotheses rather than settle one.

What we found

  • Twenty-one penetrating arterioles in the volume carry a perivascular space, and its depth is nothing like constant: from about 35 µm on one of them to over 500 on another.
  • Meningeal macrophages carry indigestible waste in large lysosomes into the arachnoid villus and appear to exocytose it through ducts into the villus lumen, which drains to the venous sinus — a route a PEGylated tracer designed to avoid macrophages would never report.
  • Leptomeningeal fibroblasts reach macrophages with their primary cilia, which is a contact between the two cell types nobody was looking for.
  • Glia limitans astrocytes carry GFAP bundles 1–2 µm across that run from the brain surface to capillaries, and some reach the villus lumen itself.

How this became the tools

Every study above was done the same way, and the way is slow. Find a cell. Decide what it is from its ultrastructure and its shape. Write the coordinate down. Do it again, four hundred times, and then count.

That loop is what the eight Jump tools automate. µJump is the step where you stand at a coordinate and say what the cell is; the coordinate, not the segmentation's nucleus id, is what identifies it — which is a rule these papers taught, because around vessels the public segmentation is often wrong. The region-box download writes the Colab notebook that cuts a block out of the volume and opens it in Blender, which is the route the leptomeningeal reconstruction took by hand: a CloudVolume cut-out, traced in TrakEM2 under FIJI, rendered in Blender.

The case for working this way at all — that the public volumes already contain more microvascular anatomy than any one laboratory will ever collect — is not mine. It is set out in Public volume electron microscopy data: an essential resource to study the brain microvasculature by Bonney, Coelho-Santos, Huang, Takeno, Kornfeld, Keller and Shih (Frontiers in Cell and Developmental Biology, 2022), and these tools are in a sense an attempt to do what that paper says is possible.

The tools: µJump (MICrONS minnie65 and minnie35, where four of these five studies were done), ηJump (the human h01 volume, the comparison in two of them), and ωJump, which carries 62 further volumes that nobody has published a soma list for.
